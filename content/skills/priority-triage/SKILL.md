# priority-triage — Eisenhower sort with an overload diagnostic

## 0. Diagnose first: genuine overload, or habitual triage?

Before sorting anything, ask: is this week actually overloaded with
important work, or has it been quietly running on triage — reacting to
whatever is about to break, week after week, with no time ever spent on
Quadrant 2 work?

- Triage (firefighting whatever's closest to failing) has a "whatever
  breaks next" time horizon, not "what matters this year." Being good at
  it doesn't compound into progress — a person can handle every fire well
  and still never reach a thesis, a paper, or an application essay.
- The tell for *habitual* triage (as opposed to one legitimately rough
  week): several weeks running with zero Quadrant 2 time. One crisis week
  is normal; a chronic pattern means triage has become the default mode
  by omission, not by necessity.
- If this is the chronic pattern: don't just run the sort below — name
  the semester/application-cycle end state first ("what does this
  semester/cycle actually need to produce?"), then let that reverse-engineer
  into what belongs in the list at all. Skipping this step and going
  straight to sorting just produces a better-organized triage list, not
  a way out of triage.
- If it's a one-off rough week (deadline pileup, exam week, etc.): skip
  straight to the sort — that's what it's for.

## 1. Sort: urgent x important

Two literal questions per task, not a vague "what matters":
1. Does this need action soon (urgent)?
2. Does this advance a core goal — degree, research output, application
   outcome (important)?

Quadrants:
- **Do First** (urgent + important): immediate, high-consequence —
  exam tomorrow, application deadline this week, PI needs a result today.
- **Schedule** (important, not urgent): thesis/research writing,
  application essays, deep reading — put a date on the calendar now,
  before it becomes Do First.
- **Delegate** (urgent, not important): time-sensitive but doesn't need
  Ethan specifically — group logistics, routine forms, scheduling.
- **Delete**: neither urgent nor important — drop it.

Keep separate matrices per domain (classes vs. research vs. applications)
rather than one blended list, and re-run the sort weekly since urgency
shifts as deadlines close in.

## 2. Watch for the mere-urgency effect

A notification, quick request, or looming deadline can make a task *feel*
more important than it is, pulling attention into Do First/Delegate at the
expense of Schedule — which is exactly the quadrant research reading,
thesis writing, and application essays live in. When a task feels urgent,
check it against question 2 (does it advance a core goal) before letting
the feeling of urgency decide its quadrant.

## 3. Cap Do First before scheduling

Cap each quadrant at roughly 10 items. If Do First alone exceeds that, the
matrix has stopped doing its job — it's just become another overwhelming
list. Overflow is the signal to push items to Delegate or Delete, not to
work longer hours. If Do First is chronically over cap, that's also a sign
to re-run step 0 — a Do First that never shrinks is triage wearing the
matrix's clothing.
